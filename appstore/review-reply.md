**1. What the app does and the problem it solves**

Unvarnished teaches people to look closely at paintings. Most art apps show a painting and a block of text, which people skim and forget. Unvarnished turns looking into a guessing game, because people remember facts they have committed to a guess about.

Each of the 100 paintings is played in five rounds. In each round the app zooms into a specific part of the painting, such as the mirror in the Arnolfini Portrait or the name Clara Peeters hid on a knife handle, and offers three statements about that detail. Only one is true. A wrong pick is greyed out and the player keeps looking until they find the true one; then every statement is marked true or false with a one-line explanation, so even a wrong guess teaches something. The rounds get harder as they go: the first are warm-ups, and the last are near misses that differ by a single name, date or number. After the fifth round the player reads a short history of the painting. Getting all five right on the first try adds the painting to the player's collection, which keeps what they learned about it.

**2. Intended users**

Adults and older teenagers who are curious about art but are not specialists: people who visit museums and want to understand what they are seeing, students taking an art history course, and people who enjoy trivia and want something more substantial than general-knowledge quizzes. The rounds are designed so that a newcomer can work out the early answers by looking, while the later rounds reward people who know the subject.

**3. The gap it fills**

Existing art apps fall into two groups. Quiz apps ask the player to name the artist or the title from a whole image, which tests recognition, not looking. "Painting a day" apps show an image with text but ask nothing of the reader. Unvarnished does something neither does: it directs attention to a specific detail of a painting, asks a question about that detail, and explains the answer. All of the content (500 rounds, 1,500 statements with explanations, and 100 painting histories) was written for this app, and the zoom point for each detail was chosen individually. The images are public-domain reproductions from the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Cleveland Museum of Art and Wikimedia Commons, credited in the app. The app has no ads, no accounts and collects no data.

*If you have not run a TestFlight beta:* We have not yet run a public TestFlight beta. The app was tested throughout development on an iPhone 13 mini and in the iOS Simulator, playing through paintings in every category, and that testing led to these changes in the submitted build:
- A wrong pick no longer reveals the true statement. It is greyed out and the player keeps looking until they find the true one.
- Each round now moves on automatically five seconds after the true statement is found, with the countdown shown inside the button.
- The later rounds were rewritten to be harder. Testing showed the true statement was often noticeably longer than the false ones and could be guessed without looking, so the content build now checks that true and false statements are of similar length from round three onward.
- The home screen now starts with a choice of period rather than a random painting.
- A short history of each painting was added after the fifth round.
- Two bugs were found and fixed: a zoomed-in painting blocked taps on the answers below it, and a swipe that started on the painting didn't scroll the screen.
- Every statement was fact-checked against museum and published sources, and six were corrected.
