## canaletto-stonemason
Canaletto painted this view of Venice around 1725, early in his career. Instead of the grand canals and ceremonies he later sold to wealthy British tourists, it shows a working corner of the city: a dusty stonemason's yard in the Campo San Vidal, littered with marble blocks. Across the Grand Canal stands the church of Santa Maria della Carità. Everyday life fills the scene, from washing hung at windows to a child who has fallen over while a woman rushes out to help. Many consider it his masterpiece. It came to the National Gallery in London from the collector Sir George Beaumont.

## soap-bubbles
Jean Siméon Chardin painted Soap Bubbles around 1733 to 1734. A young man leans out of a window, carefully blowing a large bubble through a straw, while a small child peers over the ledge to watch. The bubble, which could burst at any moment, was a traditional symbol of how brief life is. Chardin was celebrated for quiet still lifes and everyday scenes, painted with soft light and patient attention. He painted several versions of this subject; three survive, in New York, Washington and Los Angeles. This one belongs to the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

## mr-and-mrs-andrews
Thomas Gainsborough painted this portrait around 1750, when he was only about 22. Robert Andrews and his young wife Frances pose beneath an oak tree on their estate in Suffolk, with their fields stretching away under a wide summer sky. He holds a gun, a hunting dog at his feet. The landscape is as important as the sitters, a display of the land they owned. Oddly, Mrs Andrews' lap is unfinished, a patch of bare canvas, perhaps meant for a pheasant or a baby. It stayed with the family for two centuries and came to the National Gallery in 1960.

## the-swing
Jean-Honoré Fragonard painted The Swing around 1767 for a baron who asked to be shown watching his mistress on a swing. In a lush, overgrown garden, a young woman in a frothy pink dress flies high, kicking off a shoe toward her lover hidden in the bushes, while an older man pushes the swing, unaware. A statue of Cupid raises a finger to his lips. It is the most famous image of the playful, flirtatious Rococo style. A cleaning in 2021 removed yellowed varnish and revealed brighter colours. It hangs in the Wallace Collection in London.

## vigee-le-brun-straw-hat
Élisabeth Louise Vigée Le Brun painted this self-portrait after 1782, inspired by Rubens's portrait known as The Straw Hat, which she had seen in Flanders. She shows herself outdoors under a blue sky, wearing a straw hat trimmed with flowers and a feather, holding a palette and brushes. She was one of the most successful portrait painters of her time and Marie Antoinette's favourite portraitist. When the French Revolution broke out in 1789, she fled France and worked across Europe for years, painting royalty and aristocrats. This version belongs to the National Gallery in London.

## death-of-socrates
Jacques-Louis David painted this scene in 1787. Condemned to death in ancient Athens, the philosopher Socrates calmly reaches for the cup of poison hemlock while still teaching his grieving followers. The man handing him the cup turns away, unable to watch. Plato sits at the foot of the bed, shown as an old man although he was young at the time. With its clear outlines, stagecraft and moral message about dying for one's principles, the painting became a key work of Neoclassicism. It was a sensation at the Paris Salon. The Metropolitan Museum has owned it since 1931.

## manuel-osorio
Francisco de Goya painted this portrait of the young son of a Spanish count in 1787 to 1788. The boy stands in a bright red suit, holding a pet magpie on a string; in its beak the bird carries Goya's calling card. At his side, three cats stare hungrily at the bird, and a cage of finches sits nearby. The charming scene carries a hint of danger, often read as a reminder of how fragile childhood is. The boy died young, at about eight. The portrait belongs to the Metropolitan Museum of Art and is one of its best-loved works.

## death-of-marat
Jacques-Louis David painted this picture in 1793, during the French Revolution. Jean-Paul Marat, a radical journalist and David's political ally, had been stabbed in his bath by Charlotte Corday, who opposed his politics. Marat spent hours in the tub to ease a painful skin disease, and worked from there. David shows him dead, still holding Corday's letter and his pen, against a bare, empty wall. The wooden box bears a simple dedication: "To Marat, David." The painting turned a murder into a revolutionary martyrdom. It belongs to the Royal Museums of Fine Arts of Belgium in Brussels.

## third-of-may
Francisco de Goya painted this scene in 1814, after the French had been driven out of Spain. It shows the night after the uprising of 2 May 1808 in Madrid, when Napoleon's soldiers executed Spanish civilians in reprisal. The firing squad stands faceless, in a regimented line, while a lantern lights the victims. At the centre, a man in a white shirt flings his arms wide like a figure on a cross. The painting's raw horror and lack of glory made it one of the first modern images of war. Manet and Picasso later echoed it. It hangs in the Prado.

## wanderer
Caspar David Friedrich painted this image around 1818. A man in a dark coat stands with his back to us on a rocky peak, looking out over a sea of fog and distant mountains. Friedrich often used such figures seen from behind, so that we share their view and their feelings of awe and solitude. The scene is assembled from sketches of several real places, mostly in the Elbe Sandstone Mountains. It has become the defining image of German Romanticism and of the individual facing the vastness of nature. The Hamburger Kunsthalle bought it in 1970.

## raft-of-the-medusa
Théodore Géricault painted this huge canvas in 1818 to 1819. In 1816 the French navy frigate Méduse ran aground off West Africa; around 150 people were set adrift on a makeshift raft, and only 15 survived the starvation, madness and cannibalism that followed. The scandal embarrassed the French government. Géricault interviewed survivors, built a model raft and studied corpses. He shows the moment the survivors spot a tiny ship on the horizon. The painting, about five by seven metres, became a landmark of French Romanticism. Bitumen in the paint has darkened it over time. It hangs in the Louvre.

## hay-wain
John Constable painted The Hay Wain in 1821. A horse-drawn hay cart stands in the shallow River Stour near Flatford Mill in Suffolk, beside the cottage of a farmer named Willy Lott, under a sky full of summer clouds. Constable grew up here and painted the landscape all his life, making countless oil sketches outdoors, especially of skies. In England the painting found little favour at first, but at the Paris Salon of 1824 it won a gold medal and inspired French painters. Today it is one of the most popular paintings in Britain, in the National Gallery.

## liberty-leading-the-people
Eugène Delacroix painted this scene in 1830, the year of the July Revolution in Paris, when citizens rose up and overthrew King Charles X. A woman personifying Liberty, bare-breasted and wearing a red Phrygian cap, strides over a barricade of bodies holding the French tricolour and a rifle. Around her, citizens of different classes charge forward, including a boy with pistols often said to have inspired Gavroche in Les Misérables. The towers of Notre-Dame rise in the background. The French state bought it, then hid it away for years as too inflammatory. It hangs in the Louvre.

## burning-of-parliament
J. M. W. Turner watched the old Palace of Westminster burn on the night of 16 October 1834, along with huge crowds along the Thames. He made sketches and later painted two oil versions; this one is in the Cleveland Museum of Art, the other in Philadelphia. Flames and smoke pour into the night sky, reflected in the river, while onlookers crowd the bank and a bridge glows in the light. The scene let Turner do what he loved most, painting light, fire and atmosphere. The building that replaced the old palace is today's Houses of Parliament, with Big Ben.

## oxbow
Thomas Cole painted The Oxbow in 1836. From Mount Holyoke in Massachusetts, the view shows a storm clearing over wild forest on the left, while sunlit farmland curves around a loop in the Connecticut River on the right. The contrast between wilderness and cultivation reflects debates about America's changing land. Cole painted himself tiny at his easel in the foreground, and on the distant hill cleared trees form Hebrew letters that can be read as "Noah." Cole is seen as the founder of the Hudson River School of American landscape painters. The painting belongs to the Met.

## fighting-temeraire
J. M. W. Turner painted this scene in 1839. The Temeraire, a 98-gun warship that had fought at the Battle of Trafalgar in 1805, is towed up the Thames by a small, dark steam tug to be broken up. Against a blazing sunset, the pale old ship looks almost ghostly, a symbol of a passing age replaced by the new world of steam. Turner kept the painting until his death and left it to the nation. In 2005 it was voted Britain's greatest painting, and since 2020 it has appeared on the Bank of England's 20 pound note.

## burial-at-ornans
Gustave Courbet painted this enormous canvas in 1849 to 1850. It shows a funeral in Ornans, his home town in eastern France, with dozens of villagers in black gathered around an open grave beneath a raised crucifix. The mourners were real locals, including Courbet's own family. What shocked critics was its scale: about three by seven metres, a size usually kept for history paintings of heroes and gods, here given to ordinary people. Courbet made it a manifesto of Realism, painting the world as it is rather than as it should be. It hangs in the Musée d'Orsay.

## princesse-de-broglie
Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres painted this portrait of Pauline de Broglie between 1851 and 1853. She leans on a chair upholstered in gold damask, wearing a shimmering blue satin gown, lace and jewellery, every surface rendered with smooth, flawless precision. Ingres was the great champion of drawing and clear line in French painting, and his portraits of the Parisian elite are famous for their cool perfection. The princess died young, at 35, and her husband kept the portrait for the rest of his life. It came to the Metropolitan Museum with the Robert Lehman Collection in 1975.

## horse-fair
Rosa Bonheur painted The Horse Fair between 1852 and 1855. It shows a surging crowd of horses and grooms at the Paris horse market, with white horses rearing at the centre. To study the animals, Bonheur sketched at the market twice a week, dressed in men's clothes, for which she had a police permit. She is thought to have included herself among the riders. The canvas is huge, about 2.4 by 5 metres, and it made her famous in Europe and America. Cornelius Vanderbilt gave it to the Metropolitan Museum in 1887, where it remains a favourite.

## gleaners
Jean-François Millet painted The Gleaners in 1857. Three peasant women bend double in a stubble field, picking up the stray ears of wheat left after the harvest, a right allowed only to the very poor. Behind them, a huge harvest is piled high under the eye of an overseer on horseback. Millet gave these women the dignity and scale of figures in a history painting. Some critics found it threatening, a call to revolution only a few years after 1848. Today it is one of the most famous images of rural labour. It hangs in the Musée d'Orsay.

## heart-of-the-andes
Frederic Edwin Church painted this vast South American landscape in 1859, after travelling in Ecuador and Colombia. A waterfall and jungle fill the foreground, hills and valleys roll away behind, and the snowy peak of Chimborazo rises in the far distance. Church combined many sketches into one panorama, packed with precise detail. It was first shown on its own in a darkened room in New York, lit by gaslight, and thousands paid to see it, some using opera glasses. It sold for 10,000 dollars, then a record for a living American artist. It belongs to the Met.

## ninth-wave
Ivan Aivazovsky painted The Ninth Wave in 1850. After a night of storm, survivors of a shipwreck cling to a broken mast as huge waves rise around them, while the sun breaks through orange clouds. Sailors believed that every ninth wave was the biggest and most dangerous. Aivazovsky, born in Feodosia in Crimea, devoted his life to painting the sea, almost always from memory in the studio, and produced about 6,000 works. The glowing light and translucent water made this his most famous painting. It belongs to the State Russian Museum in St Petersburg.

## max-schmitt
Thomas Eakins painted his friend Max Schmitt, a champion rower, in 1871. Schmitt rests on his oars on the Schuylkill River in Philadelphia, on a calm autumn afternoon, with bridges in the distance. Eakins, himself a keen rower, appears further back in another boat, with his name painted on it. He worked out the perspective of the boats with precise drawings, combining scientific accuracy with a quiet, reflective mood. It was one of his first major paintings after studying in Paris. Today Eakins is regarded as one of America's greatest realist painters. It belongs to the Met.

## whistlers-mother
James McNeill Whistler painted his mother, Anna, in London in 1871. He called it Arrangement in Grey and Black No. 1, because he wanted it seen as a harmony of tones, like music, rather than as a sentimental portrait. She sits in profile against a grey wall, beside a curtain and one of his own etchings. She was meant to stand, but tired, so he painted her seated. In 1891 the French state bought it, the first American painting in a French national collection. It became an icon of motherhood in America. It hangs in the Musée d'Orsay.

## barge-haulers
Ilya Repin painted this scene between 1870 and 1873, after travelling along the Volga River. Eleven exhausted men, harnessed with straps, drag a barge along a sandy bank under a hot sun. Each is an individual: the leader was a former priest Repin met, and a young man in pink strains upward, not yet beaten. A steamship in the distance hints that machines would soon replace them. The painting made Repin famous and became a powerful image of human endurance and social injustice in Russia. Grand Duke Vladimir bought it. It belongs to the State Russian Museum.
