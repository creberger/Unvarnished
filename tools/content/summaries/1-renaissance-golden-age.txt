## duccio-madonna
Duccio di Buoninsegna painted this small Madonna and Child in Siena around 1300, when Italian painting was beginning to move away from the flat, formal style of Byzantine icons. Mary still sits against a field of real gold leaf, but the Child reaches up to tug at her veil, a tender, human gesture, and a painted ledge in front makes the figures feel close enough to touch. The scorched frame shows it was used for private prayer by candlelight. When the Metropolitan Museum bought it in 2004, for around 45 million dollars, it was the most expensive purchase in the museum's history.

## arnolfini
Jan van Eyck painted this double portrait in Bruges in 1434, and it remains one of the most closely studied pictures in Western art. A wealthy merchant and his wife stand hand in hand in a bedroom filled with costly things: oranges, a brass chandelier, a carpet, a little dog. Van Eyck's mastery of oil paint let him layer thin, glowing glazes, so every surface catches the light. The convex mirror on the back wall reflects two more figures in the doorway, and above it he signed the wall like a witness: "Jan van Eyck was here." Scholars still debate exactly what event it records.

## descent-from-the-cross
Rogier van der Weyden painted this altarpiece around 1435 for the crossbowmen's guild of Leuven, in what is now Belgium. Ten nearly life-size figures crowd into a shallow gold box, as if they were carved and painted sculptures in a church shrine. Christ's body is lowered from the cross, and Mary collapses in a faint that mirrors the curve of his body, joining her grief to his suffering. Tears glisten on the mourners' faces. The work was admired across Europe, copied many times, and eventually acquired by Philip II of Spain. It now hangs in the Prado in Madrid.

## garden-of-earthly-delights
Hieronymus Bosch's great triptych, painted around 1490 to 1510, is one of the strangest and most debated works in European art. Read from left to right, it moves from the Garden of Eden, through a vast paradise crowded with naked figures, giant fruit and fantastic birds, to a dark and inventive hell where musical instruments become instruments of torture. Closed, its outer panels show the world as a grey globe during creation. Nobody is sure whether it celebrates pleasure or warns against it. Philip II of Spain collected Bosch avidly, and the painting is now one of the Prado's great treasures.

## young-hare
Albrecht Dürer painted this hare in 1502, using watercolour and opaque white on paper. He built it up from broad washes to thousands of fine strokes, each following the direction of the fur, and even caught the reflection of a window in the animal's eye. At the time, studying an ordinary creature this carefully, for its own sake, was unusual. The picture became one of the most copied images in German art. Because the paper and pigments are so sensitive to light, the Albertina in Vienna rarely displays the original, usually showing a facsimile in its place.

## mona-lisa
Leonardo da Vinci began this portrait in Florence around 1503 and kept working on it for years, carrying it with him to France, where he died in 1519. The sitter is generally identified as Lisa Gherardini, wife of a Florentine merchant. Leonardo blended the shadows so softly, a technique called sfumato, that her expression seems to change as you look. Behind her, a misty, imagined landscape doesn't quite line up from one side to the other. The painting became world famous after it was stolen from the Louvre in 1911 by a museum worker and recovered two years later.

## castiglione
Raphael painted this portrait of his friend Baldassare Castiglione around 1514 to 1515. Castiglione was a diplomat and writer whose Book of the Courtier described the ideal gentleman: graceful, learned and never seeming to try too hard. Raphael gave the painting the same qualities, with a calm, direct gaze and a restrained palette of greys, blacks and creams. The relaxed pose and clasped hands, cut off by the frame, make the sitter feel present. The portrait influenced generations of painters. Rembrandt sketched it at an Amsterdam auction in 1639 and borrowed the pose for his own self-portraits.

## bacchus-and-ariadne
Titian painted this blazing mythological scene between 1520 and 1523 for the private study of Alfonso d'Este, Duke of Ferrara. Ariadne, abandoned by Theseus on the island of Naxos, turns in alarm as Bacchus, god of wine, leaps from his chariot at the very moment he falls in love with her. His noisy, drunken followers tumble in behind, and two cheetahs pull the chariot. Above her head a ring of stars shows the crown he will give her, later set in the sky. Titian used costly ultramarine generously for the sky. The National Gallery in London owns it.

## ambassadors
Hans Holbein the Younger painted this life-size double portrait in London in 1533. It shows Jean de Dinteville, French ambassador to Henry VIII, and his friend Georges de Selve, a bishop. Between them, two shelves hold globes, scientific instruments, books and a lute with a broken string, signs of learning and of a Europe divided by religious conflict. Across the floor stretches a strange, slanted shape that becomes a skull only when viewed from the side, a reminder of death. A small crucifix half hidden behind the curtain offers hope. It hangs in the National Gallery, London.

## anguissola-chess
Sofonisba Anguissola painted this scene of her three younger sisters playing chess in 1555, when she was in her early twenties. Showing educated young women at an intellectual game was itself a statement, and the relaxed, affectionate expressions were new in portraiture. The youngest sister grins at the game while a servant watches from the edge. Anguissola became one of the first women painters to win international fame, serving at the Spanish court of Philip II. She lived to about 93, and the young Anthony van Dyck visited and sketched her in her old age. It is in Poznań, Poland.

## hunters-in-the-snow
Pieter Bruegel the Elder painted this winter scene in 1565 as part of a series on the times of the year, made for an Antwerp merchant. Exhausted hunters and their dogs trudge home with a single fox, while below them villagers skate and play on frozen ponds. The jagged mountains are imagined, since Flanders is flat; Bruegel had seen the Alps on his travels. The cold, muted colours and the birds against the pale sky make the scene feel almost silent. It hangs in the Kunsthistorisches Museum in Vienna, which holds more Bruegels than anywhere else.

## harvesters
Pieter Bruegel the Elder painted The Harvesters in 1565, as another of his series on the times of the year. In the heat of late summer, peasants cut and stack golden wheat, and a group pauses for lunch in the shade of a pear tree while one man sleeps sprawled on the ground. Beyond the fields the landscape opens out to a distant bay with ships. Bruegel shows ordinary rural work with warmth and humour rather than as a backdrop for a grander story. It is the only painting from the series in the Americas, at the Metropolitan Museum since 1919.

## el-greco-assumption
El Greco painted this towering altarpiece between 1577 and 1579, soon after arriving in Toledo from Italy. It was his first major commission in Spain, made for the convent church of Santo Domingo el Antiguo. Below, the apostles crowd around Mary's empty tomb, while above she rises on a crescent moon, surrounded by angels. The elongated figures, swirling movement and vivid colours show the style that made him famous. Born on Crete and trained as an icon painter, he signed it in Greek letters. The Art Institute of Chicago has owned it since 1906.

## view-of-toledo
El Greco painted this stormy view of his adopted city around 1599 to 1600. It is one of only two landscapes he is known to have made. Toledo rises on its hill under a dark, turbulent sky, the green fields glowing with an almost unnatural light. El Greco rearranged the buildings for effect, moving the cathedral to stand beside the royal palace, the Alcázar. Rather than an accurate record, it feels like a vision of the city's spirit. Its expressive freedom made it a favourite of modern painters. It came to the Met in 1929 with the Havemeyer collection.

## supper-at-emmaus
Caravaggio painted this scene in Rome in 1601. After the Resurrection, two disciples share a meal with a stranger, and at the moment he blesses the bread they realise he is Christ. One flings his arms wide, the other starts up from his chair. Caravaggio shows Christ beardless and young, and fills the table with real, slightly bruised fruit in a basket teetering on the edge. Strong light against deep shadow makes the moment feel like theatre. His blunt realism shocked some viewers and inspired many painters. The painting hangs in the National Gallery, London.

## artemisia-catherine
Artemisia Gentileschi painted herself as Saint Catherine of Alexandria around 1615 to 1617, during her years in Florence. Catherine was a princess martyred for her faith; she wears a small crown, holds a martyr's palm and leans on the broken spiked wheel that was meant to kill her. Gentileschi was one of the most successful painters of her age and the first woman admitted to Florence's Academy of Design. For centuries her work was overshadowed or credited to men. When the National Gallery in London bought this painting in 2018, it was the gallery's first work by her.

## peeters-cheeses
Clara Peeters painted this table of food in Antwerp around 1615. She was one of the first painters in the Low Countries to specialise in still lifes of laid tables, and one of very few women working in the genre. Stacked cheeses, a stoneware jug, a glass goblet, pretzels and a Chinese porcelain bowl of almonds and raisins are seen from slightly above, every surface carefully observed. She signed her name on the silver handle of the knife at the front. Little is known about her life. The painting belongs to the Mauritshuis in The Hague.

## laughing-cavalier
Frans Hals painted this swaggering portrait in Haarlem in 1624. The sitter, whose name is unknown, was 26 according to the inscription. He isn't really laughing: the famous title was invented in the nineteenth century. His upturned moustache, cocked hat and hand on hip give him confidence, and his sleeve is embroidered with bees, arrows and flaming horns of plenty, emblems of love. Hals painted the lace and fabric with quick, loose strokes that look modern up close. Lord Hertford bought it at a Paris auction in 1865, and it hangs in the Wallace Collection, London.

## leyster-self-portrait
Judith Leyster painted this self-portrait in Haarlem around 1630, when she was about 21. She turns from her easel with an open, easy smile, as if we have just interrupted her, holding a palette and a bundle of brushes. On the easel is a laughing fiddler, the kind of lively scene she was known for. In 1633 she became one of the first women admitted to Haarlem's painters' guild, and she ran a workshop with students. For about two centuries her paintings were credited to Frans Hals. It belongs to the National Gallery of Art in Washington.

## night-watch
Rembrandt finished this enormous group portrait of an Amsterdam civic militia company in 1642. Instead of lining the men up stiffly, he showed them bursting into action, led by Captain Frans Banninck Cocq in black and his lieutenant in yellow, with a glowing girl among them carrying the company's emblem. The name came later, when darkened varnish made it look like a night scene. In 1715 it was trimmed on all sides to fit a wall in the town hall. It has survived three attacks and is the centrepiece of the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam.

## juan-de-pareja
Diego Velázquez painted this portrait in Rome in 1650. Juan de Pareja was his enslaved assistant, of Moorish descent, who ground his pigments and helped in the studio. Velázquez signed papers freeing him that same year, and Pareja went on to become a painter himself; his works are in the Prado. The portrait shows him with a steady, dignified gaze, painted with loose, quick strokes that seem strikingly modern up close. When it was exhibited at the Pantheon in Rome it caused a sensation. In 1970 the Met bought it at auction for a then-record price.

## las-meninas
Diego Velázquez painted Las Meninas in 1656 at the Spanish royal palace in Madrid. The little Infanta Margarita stands at the centre among her maids of honour, dwarfs, a chaperone and a sleepy dog. Velázquez includes himself at a huge canvas, looking out at us, while a mirror on the back wall reflects the king and queen, who seem to stand where we are. The painting plays with who is watching whom, and has been analysed endlessly. Picasso painted 58 variations of it in 1957. It is one of the treasures of the Prado.

## view-of-delft
Johannes Vermeer painted his home town around 1660 to 1661, looking across the water at its walls, gates and sunlit roofs under a vast sky of clouds. Shadows cover the nearer buildings, while sunlight picks out the tower of the Nieuwe Kerk in the distance. Vermeer adjusted the view, stretching reflections and shifting buildings to make it more harmonious. It is one of only a few cityscapes he painted. The novelist Marcel Proust called it the most beautiful painting in the world. The Mauritshuis in The Hague bought it at auction in 1822.

## milkmaid
Johannes Vermeer painted The Milkmaid around 1658, when he was in his mid-twenties. A kitchen maid pours milk into an earthenware bowl, so absorbed that she seems unaware of us. Light from the window falls across her yellow bodice and her apron of costly ultramarine, and Vermeer dabbed tiny dots of pale paint on the bread so it seems to sparkle. Scans show he first painted a laundry basket where the foot warmer now sits. The painting is small, only 45.5 by 41 centimetres. The Rijksmuseum bought it in 1908, and it is one of its most beloved works.

## girl-with-a-pearl-earring
Johannes Vermeer painted this girl around 1665. It isn't a portrait of a known person but a tronie, a study of a face, costume or expression. She turns to look over her shoulder, lips slightly parted, wearing an exotic blue and yellow turban painted with expensive ultramarine. The famous pearl is just a few strokes of paint, with no hook to hang it from. A 2018 study found traces of eyelashes and showed that the dark background was once a green curtain. It is the star of the Mauritshuis in The Hague and inspired a novel and a film.

## rembrandt-at-63
Rembrandt painted this self-portrait in 1669, the last year of his life. He made around 80 self-portraits in paint, drawing and etching, tracking his face from a confident young man to old age. Here he looks out with tired, steady eyes, his face built up in thick paint that captures every wrinkle. X-rays show that he first painted himself holding brushes, then changed it to clasped hands. Bankruptcy and the deaths of loved ones had marked his later years, and the portrait seems honest about all of it. It hangs in the National Gallery, London.

## ruysch-flowers
Rachel Ruysch painted this bouquet around 1700. The flowers spill and twist out of the vase against a dark background, each petal and leaf carefully observed. The arrangement could never have existed, since the flowers bloom in different seasons; painters assembled such bouquets from studies made over many months. Ruysch was the daughter of a famous anatomist and botanist, and she painted for nearly 70 years. She became court painter to the Elector Palatine in Düsseldorf, and in her lifetime her paintings sold for more than Rembrandt's had. This one belongs to the Mauritshuis in The Hague.
