## impression-sunrise
Claude Monet painted this view of the harbour at Le Havre, where he grew up, in 1872. An orange sun rises through blue morning haze, reflected in the water, while small dark boats are sketched in with a few quick strokes. When it was shown in 1874 at an independent exhibition in Paris, a critic mocked its title, calling the whole group "Impressionists," and the name stuck. The painting captures a fleeting moment of light rather than precise detail, which was exactly the point. It was stolen in 1985 and recovered in 1990. It hangs in the Musée Marmottan Monet, Paris.

## the-cradle
Berthe Morisot painted The Cradle in 1872. Her sister Edma watches over her sleeping baby daughter, Blanche, through a sheer white gauze veil draped over the cradle. The gauze is painted with light, quick strokes, and the mother's gaze and pose create a quiet intimacy. Morisot was a founding member of the Impressionists, and she was the only woman in their first exhibition in 1874, where this painting was shown. She often painted women and children in domestic settings, subjects her male colleagues rarely explored as closely. She married Eugène Manet, Édouard's brother. It hangs in the Musée d'Orsay.

## dance-class
Edgar Degas painted The Dance Class in 1874. Young ballerinas in white tutus wait, stretch, chat and fidget in a rehearsal room while the elderly ballet master Jules Perrot leans on a tall stick he used to beat time. One dancer scratches her back; another adjusts her dress. Degas was fascinated by the unglamorous moments behind the performance, and dancers fill more than half his work. Though the scene looks spontaneous, he composed it carefully in his studio from models and sketches. The steep, slanting floor pulls us into the room. It belongs to the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

## paris-street-rainy-day
Gustave Caillebotte painted this large view of a Paris intersection in 1877. Well-dressed people with umbrellas cross the wet cobbles of the Place de Dublin, near the Gare Saint-Lazare, among the wide new boulevards and stone apartment blocks built under Baron Haussmann's rebuilding of the city. A tall lamppost divides the composition in two. The figures at the front are almost life-size, and the precise, cool style differs from his Impressionist friends' looser brushwork. Caillebotte was also their patron, funding exhibitions and leaving his collection to France. The Art Institute of Chicago owns it.

## boating-party
Pierre-Auguste Renoir painted the Luncheon of the Boating Party in 1880 to 1881. Fourteen friends relax after lunch on the balcony of the Maison Fournaise, a restaurant on the Seine at Chatou, west of Paris. They talk, drink and flirt under a striped awning, the scene full of dappled light and warm colour. The woman playing with a little dog at the left is Aline Charigot, who later married Renoir, and the painter Gustave Caillebotte sits at the bottom right. Duncan Phillips bought it in 1923, and it is the centrepiece of the Phillips Collection in Washington.

## folies-bergere
Édouard Manet painted A Bar at the Folies-Bergère in 1882, his last major work before his death the following year. A barmaid named Suzon stands behind a marble counter of bottles and oranges at a famous Paris music hall. Behind her, a mirror reflects the glittering, crowded hall and a trapeze artist's green boots, but the reflection doesn't match: her image is shifted to the right and faces a man in a top hat who isn't visible in front. Her detached expression has prompted endless interpretations. It belongs to The Courtauld in London.

## grande-jatte
Georges Seurat spent about two years, from 1884 to 1886, painting this scene of Parisians relaxing on an island in the Seine. Couples stroll, a woman walks a monkey on a leash, and people sit by the water in the afternoon sun. The whole canvas is built from tiny dots and dashes of pure colour, a technique called pointillism, meant to blend in the viewer's eye. Seurat made dozens of preparatory studies and later added a painted border of dots. The figures' stiff stillness gives the scene a strange calm. It belongs to the Art Institute of Chicago.

## childs-bath
Mary Cassatt painted The Child's Bath in 1893. A woman in a boldly striped dress holds a small child on her lap and washes its feet in a basin, both of them looking down at the water. The high viewpoint, flattened space and strong patterns show the influence of Japanese woodblock prints, which Cassatt had seen in a large exhibition in Paris. An American living in France, she was invited by Degas to exhibit with the Impressionists, and she made mothers and children her central subject. She never married or had children. It belongs to the Art Institute of Chicago.

## stacks-of-wheat
Claude Monet painted Stacks of Wheat (End of Summer) in 1890 to 1891, in a field near his house at Giverny. It belongs to a series of about 25 paintings of the same stacks, painted at different times of day and in different seasons and weather. Monet wanted to capture how light and atmosphere change what we see, even with an ordinary subject. The shadows are full of colour rather than grey. When he exhibited the series in Paris in 1891, it sold out within days and made him wealthy. The Art Institute of Chicago owns six of them.

## bridge-over-water-lilies
Claude Monet painted this view of his garden at Giverny in 1899. A pale green Japanese-style footbridge arches over a pond crowded with water lilies, framed by dense foliage. Monet designed the garden himself, diverting a stream to create the pond, and collected Japanese prints that inspired the bridge. For the rest of his life the pond was his main subject; he made about 250 paintings of water lilies. Later, cataracts changed how he saw colour, and his paintings became redder and more abstract. This early version belongs to the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

## madame-x
John Singer Sargent painted this portrait of Virginie Gautreau, a famous Paris socialite, in 1883 to 1884. She stands in profile in a sleek black gown, her pale skin set against a dark background, a small crescent tiara in her hair. When it was shown at the Paris Salon in 1884, one strap had slipped from her shoulder, and the scandal was so great that Sargent repainted it and moved to London. He kept the painting for over thirty years. In 1916 he sold it to the Metropolitan Museum, calling it the best thing he had done.

## spoliarium
Juan Luna painted Spoliarium in 1884, while studying in Rome. The title refers to the chamber beneath a Roman arena where dead gladiators were dragged and stripped. In the gloom, the bodies are hauled away, while an old man searches among them and a woman weeps. The canvas is about four by seven and a half metres, the largest painting in the Philippines. It won a gold medal at the Madrid Exposition of 1884, a triumph celebrated by Filipino reformers such as José Rizal. It hangs in the National Museum of Fine Arts in Manila.

## sunflowers
Vincent van Gogh painted this still life in Arles in 1888, to decorate the bedroom of his friend Paul Gauguin, who was coming to stay. Fifteen sunflowers, some fresh and some wilting and dropping petals, fill a yellow vase against a yellow background, almost entirely in shades of a single colour. He signed it "Vincent" on the vase. He used chrome yellow, a bright new pigment that can darken over time. Sunflowers became his signature subject, and he painted several versions. This one has belonged to the National Gallery in London since 1924.

## straw-hat-self-portrait
Vincent van Gogh painted this self-portrait in Paris in 1887, where he lived with his brother Theo. Under a straw hat, his face and jacket are built from short strokes of contrasting colour, showing what he had learned from the Impressionists and pointillists since leaving the Netherlands. Short of money and unable to pay models, he painted more than 35 self-portraits. To save canvas he painted this one on the back of an earlier picture, The Potato Peeler, from his darker Dutch period. It belongs to the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

## the-bedroom
Vincent van Gogh painted his own bedroom in the Yellow House in Arles three times. This second version, from 1889, belongs to the Art Institute of Chicago; the others are in Amsterdam and Paris. A wooden bed, two chairs and a small table sit in a room drawn with deliberately skewed perspective. He wanted it to suggest rest and simplicity. He paired things, two chairs and two pillows, perhaps hoping for company. The walls now look blue, but research shows they were violet, and the red in the paint has faded. His own paintings hang on the walls.

## starry-night
Vincent van Gogh painted The Starry Night in June 1889, while staying at an asylum in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence. It is based on the view from his window before dawn, but painted from memory during the day and transformed by imagination. A dark cypress flames up in the foreground, a quiet village sleeps below, and the sky swirls with glowing stars and a crescent moon. The church steeple looks Dutch, perhaps a memory of home. He died the following year. The painting has belonged to the Museum of Modern Art in New York since 1941.

## card-players
Paul Cézanne painted a series of five Card Players in the early 1890s; this version belongs to the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Farm workers from his family's estate near Aix-en-Provence sit around a table, absorbed in their game, while another man stands watching. No money is on the table; they seem to play for its own sake. Cézanne built the figures from solid blocks of colour, giving them a monumental stillness. His approach to structure would influence Cubism. In 2011 another version from the series sold to Qatar for about 250 million dollars, then a record.

## moulin-rouge
Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec painted At the Moulin Rouge in 1892 to 1895. It shows customers around a table in the famous Paris nightclub, where he was a regular. In the background the dancer La Goulue fixes her hair, and Lautrec himself appears, small, walking beside his tall cousin. At the right edge, a woman's face looms, lit an eerie green by the gaslight. At some point a strip containing that face was cut off and later put back. Lautrec's bold cropping and colour owe much to Japanese prints. It belongs to the Art Institute of Chicago.

## surprised
Henri Rousseau painted Tiger in a Tropical Storm (Surprised!) in 1891, the first of his famous jungle scenes. A tiger crouches in tall grass as lightning flashes and wind and rain lash the leaves, the rain shown as thin silver streaks. Rousseau never left France; he studied plants in the botanical garden and animals in books and at the zoo in Paris. A toll collector by profession, nicknamed "Le Douanier," he was self-taught, and critics mocked his flat, naive style. Later, Picasso and the avant-garde admired him. It hangs in the National Gallery, London.

## sleeping-gypsy
Henri Rousseau painted The Sleeping Gypsy in 1897. Under a full moon in a silent desert, a lion sniffs at a wandering musician asleep beside her mandolin and water jug, but doesn't attack. The flat colours, crisp outlines and dreamlike calm give the scene a strange, poetic stillness. Rousseau offered it to his home town of Laval, which declined to buy it. The painting disappeared from view for years and was rediscovered in the 1920s, when the Surrealists admired its dream logic. It belongs to the Museum of Modern Art in New York.

## the-scream
Edvard Munch painted the first version of The Scream in 1893, in tempera and crayon on cardboard. A figure with a skull-like face clutches its head on a road above the Oslo fjord, under a blood-red, swirling sky. Munch described a walk at sunset when he felt "an infinite scream passing through nature." In faint pencil in the sky someone wrote "can only have been painted by a madman"; in 2021 researchers confirmed it was Munch himself. He made several versions. This one was stolen in 1994 and recovered. It belongs to the National Museum in Oslo.

## where-do-we-come-from
Paul Gauguin painted this huge canvas in Tahiti in 1897 to 1898, at a time of illness, poverty and grief after the death of his daughter. Read from right to left, it moves from a sleeping baby through adults in the prime of life to an old woman near death, with a blue idol watching over the scene. Gauguin wrote the title, three great questions, in the top left corner. He saw it as his testament, a summary of everything he believed about art and life. It belongs to the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston.

## gulf-stream
Winslow Homer painted The Gulf Stream in 1899, after trips to the Caribbean. A lone Black sailor lies on the deck of a small boat that has lost its mast, drifting in rough water. Sharks circle around him, a waterspout rises on the horizon, and a distant ship may or may not see him. Sugar cane spills from the hold. The sailor seems strangely calm. Viewers asked what would happen to him, and Homer joked that he would be rescued. The painting is now seen as one of his masterpieces. The Metropolitan Museum bought it in 1906.

## hammershoi-interior
Vilhelm Hammershøi painted this interior of his own apartment, at Strandgade 30 in Copenhagen, in 1900. A woman, probably his wife Ida, stands by a tall window in a nearly bare grey room, with a white door to the right and a patch of sunlight on the floor. Hammershøi painted the rooms of this flat about 60 times, often with Ida seen from behind, using a muted palette of greys and whites. The stillness and emptiness earned him the name "the painter of silence." Long overlooked, he is now one of Denmark's most admired artists.
