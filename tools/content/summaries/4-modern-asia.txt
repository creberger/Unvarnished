## woman-with-a-hat
Henri Matisse painted this portrait of his wife, Amélie, in 1905. Her face is streaked with green, blue and orange, and her enormous hat is a riot of loose, bright patches of colour. Matisse used colour for its emotional force rather than to describe what he saw. When it was shown at the Salon d'Automne in Paris that year, a critic called Matisse and his friends "les fauves," the wild beasts, and Fauvism was born. Gertrude and Leo Stein bought it, launching their famous collection. It belongs to the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art.

## af-klint-adulthood
Hilma af Klint painted The Ten Largest series in 1907, years before the famous pioneers of abstract art. These huge paintings, over three metres tall, trace the stages of human life from childhood to old age; No. 7 represents adulthood. Petal shapes, spirals, circles and letters float on a lilac ground. Af Klint, a trained Swedish painter, believed spirit guides she contacted in séances directed the work. She asked that these paintings not be shown until at least 20 years after her death. Exhibitions since the 1980s have transformed the story of abstract art. They belong to the Hilma af Klint Foundation.

## the-kiss
Gustav Klimt painted The Kiss in 1907 to 1908, at the height of his "golden period." A couple embraces at the edge of a flower meadow, wrapped in a single golden robe; his half is patterned with black and white rectangles, hers with circles and flowers. Klimt used real gold leaf, inspired by the Byzantine mosaics he had seen in Ravenna. The woman's eyes are closed, her face turned toward us. The Austrian state bought the painting in 1908, before it was even finished, and it has hung in Vienna ever since. It belongs to the Belvedere.

## adele-bloch-bauer
Gustav Klimt spent about three years on this portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer, completed in 1907. A Viennese patron and the wife of a sugar industrialist, she sits in a shimmering field of gold and silver leaf, her dress covered with eye-like symbols and spirals. In 1938 the Nazis seized the painting from her family. After a long legal battle, her niece Maria Altmann won it back from Austria in 2006, and it was sold for 135 million dollars to Ronald Lauder. The story inspired the film Woman in Gold. It hangs in the Neue Galerie in New York.

## harmony-in-red
Henri Matisse painted The Dessert: Harmony in Red in 1908 for the Moscow collector Sergei Shchukin. A woman arranges fruit on a table in a room where the red wall and red tablecloth share the same blue pattern of plants and baskets, so the space flattens into decoration. Through the window, a garden of trees in blossom offers a cool green contrast. Matisse first painted it in blue, as Harmony in Blue, then repainted it red. After the Russian Revolution, Shchukin's collection was taken by the state. It belongs to the Hermitage in St Petersburg.

## street-dresden
Ernst Ludwig Kirchner painted Street, Dresden in 1908. Crowds with masklike faces push along a lurid pink street, a woman in a huge hat at the front and a tram behind. The colours and distortions express the anxiety and alienation of modern city life rather than its appearance. Kirchner co-founded Die Brücke, The Bridge, a group of German Expressionists who wanted art to be raw and direct. The Nazis later called his work "degenerate" and removed it from museums, and Kirchner took his own life in 1938. It belongs to the Museum of Modern Art in New York.

## sorolla-seashore
Joaquín Sorolla painted Strolling along the Seashore in 1909. His wife, Clotilde, and their daughter María walk along a sunny beach at Valencia in long white dresses, the wind tugging at their veils and hats. Sorolla painted outdoors, quickly, to catch the blazing Mediterranean light, and his whites are full of blues, purples and yellows. He was the most celebrated Spanish painter of his day, famous in Europe and America for scenes of the sea and of his family. The painting hangs in the Museo Sorolla, his former house and studio in Madrid.

## city-rises
Umberto Boccioni painted The City Rises in 1910. A giant red horse and straining workers blur into a whirl of energy on a building site on the edge of Milan, where new factories and a power station are rising. Boccioni was a leading figure of Futurism, an Italian movement that celebrated speed, machines, the modern city and even violence, and rejected the art of the past. He wanted the painting to show movement and noise rather than a still moment. He died in 1916 after falling from a horse during army training. It belongs to MoMA.

## blue-horses
Franz Marc painted The Large Blue Horses in 1911. Three blue horses with sweeping, curved bodies stand together before red and yellow hills. Marc gave colours meanings: blue stood for the spiritual and the masculine. He loved animals as images of purity and harmony with nature. That year he co-founded Der Blaue Reiter, The Blue Rider, in Munich with Wassily Kandinsky, a group devoted to the spiritual in art. Marc was killed at Verdun in 1916, aged 36, and the Nazis later called his work degenerate. It belongs to the Walker Art Center in Minneapolis.

## gray-tree
Piet Mondrian painted Gray Tree in 1911. The branches of a tree spread across the canvas in rhythmic grey and black curves, almost dissolving into a pattern of lines and planes. It belongs to a series of tree paintings in which Mondrian moved step by step from realistic landscape toward pure abstraction, influenced by Cubism, which he saw in Paris. Within a few years, the curves would become the straight black lines and primary colours of his famous later work. When he moved to Paris he dropped an "a" from his name. It belongs to the Kunstmuseum Den Haag.

## gris-picasso
Juan Gris painted this portrait of Pablo Picasso in 1912. Picasso's head and body are broken into faceted planes of blue, grey and brown, and he holds a palette. Gris was a fellow Spaniard and neighbour in the same Paris studio building, and he wrote a dedication on the canvas: "Homage to Pablo Picasso." The portrait shows Cubism, the movement Picasso and Braque invented, at its most clear and orderly. It was the painting Gris chose for his first public exhibition. Gris died in 1927, aged only 40. It belongs to the Art Institute of Chicago.

## schiele-physalis
Egon Schiele painted this self-portrait in 1912, aged 22. He turns his head sharply, staring sideways out of the picture, his face drawn with angular lines and strange colours. Beside him grows a physalis, or Chinese lantern plant, with red husks. It was painted as a pair with a portrait of his partner, Wally Neuzil, and the two hang side by side. Schiele's raw, expressive style shocked Vienna and influenced Expressionism. He died of flu in 1918, at 28, three days after his wife. It belongs to the Leopold Museum in Vienna.

## composition-vii
Wassily Kandinsky painted Composition VII in 1913, after making more than 30 drawings, watercolours and oil studies. The final canvas, about two by three metres, was then painted in just a few days. An explosion of colours, lines and shapes swirls across it, with no recognisable objects, though themes such as the Flood, the Resurrection and the Last Judgement lie behind it. Kandinsky compared colours to musical notes and gave his most important works musical titles, painting ten Compositions in all. It is often called his masterpiece. It belongs to the Tretyakov Gallery in Moscow.

## black-square
Kazimir Malevich painted Black Square in 1915 and showed it at an exhibition in Petrograd, hung high in a corner where Russian homes traditionally placed a religious icon. A black square on a white ground, it was meant as a zero point for painting, freed from depicting the world. Malevich called his movement Suprematism. Over time the black paint has cracked, and researchers have found an earlier painting underneath. He painted four Black Squares in all. Under Stalin, such abstract art was suppressed. This first version belongs to the Tretyakov Gallery in Moscow.

## jack-pine
Tom Thomson painted The Jack Pine in 1916 to 1917, from a sketch made in Algonquin Park, Ontario, where he spent his summers as a guide and painter. A twisted pine is silhouetted against a glowing sunset, over a still lake painted in flat bands of colour. The bold design and colour came to define the Canadian wilderness. Thomson drowned in Canoe Lake in July 1917, aged 39, in circumstances that are still debated. His friends went on to form the Group of Seven. It belongs to the National Gallery of Canada in Ottawa.

## the-city-leger
Fernand Léger painted The City in 1919, soon after serving in the First World War, where he was gassed at Verdun. The war gave him a fascination with machines and modern technology. The large canvas is a crowded collage of flat, bright shapes: scaffolding, stencilled letters like those on posters, a purple pole and robotic figures climbing stairs. It captures the noise, speed and visual chaos of the modern city, like a billboard. Léger later worked on murals, film and stage design. It belongs to the Philadelphia Museum of Art.

## jeanne-hebuterne
Amedeo Modigliani painted Jeanne Hébuterne, his partner and the mother of his daughter, many times; this portrait dates from 1919. She rests her head on her hand, with Modigliani's signature long neck, oval face and almond-shaped eyes, influenced by African and ancient Cycladic sculpture. The couple lived in poverty in Paris. Modigliani died of tuberculosis in January 1920, aged 35, and two days later Jeanne, heavily pregnant, took her own life. She was 21. His work became valuable only after his death. It belongs to the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

## twittering-machine
Paul Klee made Twittering Machine in 1922, using an oil-transfer drawing technique, like carbon paper, combined with watercolour. Thin black lines draw four strange birds perched on a wire attached to a hand crank, over a misty blue-to-pink background. It is playful and unsettling at once, a mechanical toy that perhaps traps the birds into singing. Klee taught at the Bauhaus. In 1937 the Nazis seized the work from a German museum as "degenerate," and the Museum of Modern Art in New York bought it in 1939. Today it is one of his best-known images.

## several-circles
Wassily Kandinsky painted Several Circles in 1926, while teaching at the Bauhaus in Germany. Overlapping coloured circles of many sizes float on a dark background, mixing where they cross like transparent films, while a large black circle glows with a blue halo. Kandinsky called the circle the most modest yet the most assertive of forms, and saw it as a link to the cosmos. The painting looks like a view of planets and stars, though it is purely abstract. Solomon R. Guggenheim collected Kandinsky widely, and the painting belongs to the Guggenheim Museum in New York.

## mondrian-red-blue-yellow
Piet Mondrian painted Composition II in Red, Blue and Yellow in 1930. A large red square dominates a white grid divided by thick black lines, balanced by a blue block at the bottom left and a small yellow one at the bottom right. Mondrian limited himself to primary colours, black, white and grey, and to horizontal and vertical lines, seeking a universal harmony. He split from a fellow De Stijl artist who introduced diagonals. His style later influenced fashion, design and architecture. He moved to New York in 1940 and died there in 1944. It belongs to the Kunsthaus Zürich.

## fayum-portrait
This portrait of a young woman was painted in Egypt around 90 to 120 AD, when the country was part of the Roman Empire. It was placed over the face of her mummy, so that her likeness would last into the afterlife. Hundreds of these portraits have been found, many in the Fayum region. The artist painted in encaustic, pigment mixed with hot wax, which has kept the colours fresh for nearly 2,000 years. Her large, lifelike eyes, gold wreath and red tunic make her feel startlingly present. It belongs to the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

## night-shining-white
Han Gan painted this horse around 750 in China, during the Tang dynasty. It shows Night-Shining White, a favourite horse of the Emperor Xuanzong, tethered to a post and straining with energy, drawn in confident ink lines. Han Gan was the most celebrated horse painter of his time. Over the centuries, emperors, collectors and scholars added their red seals and written comments to the scroll, recording who owned or admired it; the marks are now part of the work. It is one of the oldest Chinese paintings in a Western museum. It belongs to the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

## great-wave
Katsushika Hokusai made Under the Wave off Kanagawa, known as The Great Wave, around 1830 to 1832, when he was about 70. It is a woodblock print from his series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji. A towering wave with claw-like foam curls over three boats of rowers, while tiny Mount Fuji sits calmly in the distance. Hokusai used Prussian blue, a vivid new imported pigment. Thousands of impressions were printed and sold cheaply. It became one of the most famous images in the world and deeply influenced European artists. This impression belongs to the Metropolitan Museum.

## sudden-shower
Utagawa Hiroshige made this woodblock print in 1857, as part of his series One Hundred Famous Views of Edo, the city now called Tokyo. People hurry across the Ōhashi bridge over the Sumida River as a sudden summer downpour sweeps in, the rain shown as fine dark lines at two angles. The steep bridge and the misty far bank give the scene depth and drama. The print was hugely popular, and Vincent van Gogh copied it in oil in 1887. It shows the Japanese art that shaped Impressionism. This impression belongs to the Metropolitan Museum.
